How the order is worked out, and how far to trust it

The calculation

  • What goes in. Every payment between two different industries where both sides are registered in the region, added up over the whole period, January 2019 to September 2026. It uses the value of payments, with hidden months held at their average. Payments to and from the rest of the UK play no part.
  • What comes out. Each industry gets a level. The levels are chosen so that, as far as the payments allow, a buyer sits one level above each of its suppliers, and big payments count for more than small ones. Precisely: they make the sum, over every link, of the payment times (buyer's level minus supplier's level minus one) squared as small as it can be. These are called trophic levels, after the food webs they were first used on.
  • Where zero is. Levels are measured from the average industry, weighted by size. Height on the chart is the level, give or take a few pixels so that circles at the same level can sit side by side.
  • One order for the whole period. Circles don't move as time plays, so everything that moves is a change in payments. Pin an industry to see how its level drifts, worked out on the 12 months to each month. The Columns option "By how much its place shifts" lines industries up by the standard deviation of that drifting level, steadiest on the left.
  • A plainer way to think of it. The order is almost the same as ranking industries by the balance of their dealings with other industries here: how much of it is paying out, and how much is being paid (the two rankings correlate ). The level adds one thing: who you buy from matters, not only how much.
  • Goods and services view. The same order, turned upside down.

What to keep in mind

  • Small gaps mean little. The whole order spans only about levels, and an industry's level wobbles by about from month to month. Read top, middle and bottom, not fine rankings.
  • "Supplier" means "gets paid". Landlords, insurers and councils collecting rates are suppliers here.
  • The top is partly who's missing. Households aren't in the data, and public money mostly arrives from outside the region. Industries that sell to the public or are publicly funded have few paying customers left in the data, so they look like pure buyers.
  • It's the order inside the region only. An industry that buys mostly from elsewhere in the UK, or sells mostly to it, is placed on the small part of its dealings that stays here.
  • Hidden figures shape it. of monthly figures belong to links ONS never publishes, and those links count as nothing. Using published months only gives single months that correlate with their whole period, against with held values.
  • Value and number of payments disagree. An order built on the number of payments correlates with this one, and with the input-output table, against for value. Value is used because across the regions it tracks the table better.
  • Small industries are unreliable. The with under £0.5m a month are drawn hollow and held inside the range of the rest.
  • Intermediaries are left out of it unless switched in. Switching them in barely moves the others (their order correlates either way).

What this shows, and what it doesn't

  • Payments, not trade. These are Bacs and Faster Payments between businesses. Read a link as a sign that two industries are connected, not as a measure of what they sell each other. Rent, insurance premiums and business rates are in here alongside purchases.
  • Goods and services are an inference. Switching "Flows of" to goods and services turns every payment round and the order upside down, on the assumption that each payment bought something that travelled the other way. Nothing in the data says what was bought, and for rent, insurance, rates and refunds nothing travelled back.
  • Registered offices, not where the work happens. A payment is placed where the paying and receiving organisations are registered. A firm headquartered elsewhere pays from elsewhere. That's why this page is about payments where both sides are in the region: no head office can move those.
  • Households aren't in it. Industries that sell to the public, or are publicly funded from outside the region, show here mostly as buyers. That's part of why hospitality, health and education tend to sit towards the top.
  • of the monthly figures here are hidden, and hidden doesn't mean small: a figure is withheld when few organisations are behind it or one dominates. A hidden month is shown at the average of that link's published months, marked with a dashed line. of monthly figures belong to links never published at all, so those links are missing. is one.
  • Growth over time may be coverage. The page opens on each month's shares so that a rising total doesn't swamp everything else.
  • The order is a gradient, not tiers. About of the money runs down it. The UK's input-output tables show the same loose order.
  • Payment groups are a statistical grouping: industries that pay each other more than their sizes would predict, in most years. Here they have been looked at and named by hand.For this region nobody has looked them over, and each is labelled by its largest members. industries here don't sit firmly in any group.
  • Intermediaries (banking and finance, financial support services, information services, office and business support) are left out unless switched in. Card takings and platform payouts pass through them, so their links are partly money passing through.
  • Nominal pounds, not seasonally adjusted. These are official statistics in development and are revised between editions. Values are rounded to £1,000 and numbers of payments to 100.

Source: Office for National Statistics, Industry-to-industry payment flows by region, UK, from Pay.UK data (editions of 17 September and 22 October 2026).
